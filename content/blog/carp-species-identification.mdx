Not every carp is the same fish. Anglers use the word "carp" for one species with several scale patterns, for ornamental fish like koi, and for several unrelated-looking species from Asia. On a mixed fishery you might catch three or four of them in one session.

Telling them apart matters. Fishery records, match rules and your own catch log all depend on getting the species right, and some fish, such as grass carp, need slightly different tactics.

This guide covers every carp you're likely to see on the bank, how to identify each one, and the features that sort them out quickly.

---

## The Quick Checks: Barbels, Dorsal Fin and Scales

Three features identify most carp in seconds.

**1. Barbels.** Look at the mouth. The common carp and all its scale forms (mirror, leather, linear) have **two pairs of barbels**: a short pair on the upper lip and a longer pair at the corners of the mouth. Crucian carp, goldfish, grass carp, silver carp, bighead carp and black carp have **none**.

**2. The dorsal fin.** Common carp and crucian carp have a **long dorsal fin** that runs along much of the back. Grass, silver, bighead and black carp have a **short dorsal fin**, set roughly in the middle of the back.

**3. Scales.** Even rows of scales, scattered large scales, a single line of scales or no scales at all point to different scale forms of the common carp. Tiny scales point to silver or bighead carp. Large scales with dark edges that make a net-like pattern point to grass carp.

## Common Carp (Cyprinus carpio)

The common carp is the fish most anglers mean when they say "carp". It is native to the Ponto-Caspian region, including the Danube and the rivers draining into the Black and Caspian Seas. It has been farmed and moved around for centuries and now lives on every continent except Antarctica. In Britain it is an introduced species, probably brought over in the Middle Ages for food.

**How to identify it:**
- Two pairs of barbels around a mouth that extends downward when feeding
- Long dorsal fin with a strong, serrated spine at the front
- Even, regular rows of scales covering the whole body
- Colour usually bronze, gold or olive on the back, paler on the belly

Wild-type common carp, sometimes called "wildies", are long and slim. Most fishery carp come from farmed strains that were bred to be deeper in the body, so they look much rounder.

The term "European carp" usually refers to this species too, especially in Australia and North America, where it is invasive.

## Mirror, Leather, Linear and Fully Scaled Carp

These are **not separate species**. They are all *Cyprinus carpio*, with different scale patterns produced by two genes. The patterns were selected by carp farmers in Europe from at least the 18th century onwards, because fish with fewer scales were easier to prepare for the table.

| Scale form | What it looks like |
|------------|--------------------|
| **Common** | Regular rows of scales over the whole body |
| **Mirror** | A few large, irregular scales scattered over the body, often along the back and near the tail |
| **Linear** | A neat single row of scales along the lateral line, sometimes with a row along the back |
| **Leather** | Few or no scales, often just a small number near the base of the dorsal fin |
| **Fully scaled mirror** | Covered in scales like a common, but the scales are uneven in size and do not sit in regular rows |

**How the genes work.** One gene decides whether the fish has full scaling or reduced "mirror" scaling. A second gene reduces scaling further, turning a common into a linear and a mirror into a leather. Inheriting two copies of that second gene is lethal, so it never appears in its double form. This is why breeding two leathers together does not give you only leathers.

**Telling a fully scaled mirror from a common.** Look at the rows. On a common carp the scales line up neatly along the body like roof tiles. On a fully scaled mirror the scales are mixed sizes and break the pattern.

Many anglers' personal bests are mirrors because many fisheries stock them, not because mirrors grow bigger. Growth depends on the strain of fish, food supply and water temperature.

## Koi and Ghost Carp

**Koi** are ornamental carp bred in Japan from the 19th century onwards for their colours: white, red, orange, yellow, black and metallic forms. Scientists still debate whether they belong to the common carp or the closely related Amur carp (*Cyprinus rubrofuscus*) from East Asia. On the bank, they are recognised by their colour and the same two pairs of barbels as a common carp.

**Ghost carp** are usually koi crossed with common or mirror carp. They have a ghostly grey, white or yellow colour, often with a metallic sheen, and the body shape of a carp. They fight and feed like any other carp and are popular in UK fisheries.

If you see a "coy carp" listed on a fishery, it almost always means koi.

## Grass Carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella)

Grass carp are native to eastern Asia, from the Amur River southwards. They are a different species from the common carp and in a different genus, although both are in the carp family, Cyprinidae.

**How to identify it:**
- **No barbels**
- Long, rounded, torpedo-shaped body
- Short dorsal fin in the middle of the back
- Large scales with dark edges that give a net-like pattern
- Wide, blunt head, with the mouth at the front rather than pointing down

**Diet.** Adult grass carp eat mostly plants, which is why they have been stocked around the world to control weed. They also take angler baits. Sweetcorn, bread and floating baits are well known for catching them, and they take boilies on many waters.

**Breeding.** In the wild, grass carp need long, flowing rivers to breed, because their eggs drift downstream as they develop. They rarely breed successfully in UK stillwaters. In England and Wales, stocking them needs permission, and many fisheries use them as a weed management tool.

**Fight.** Grass carp are known for long, fast runs and for staying calm on the unhooking mat, then thrashing suddenly. Keep hold of them carefully and stay close to the mat.

## Silver Carp and Bighead Carp

Both are filter feeders from eastern Asia. They feed on plankton rather than grubbing on the bottom, so they seldom take normal carp baits. They are rarely found in UK waters, but they are a major invasive problem in North America.

**Silver carp (Hypophthalmichthys molitrix):**
- Silver body with very small scales
- Eye set low on the head, below the line of the mouth
- Upturned mouth
- A sharp keel along the belly
- Famous for leaping out of the water when boat engines disturb them

**Bighead carp (Hypophthalmichthys nobilis):**
- Very large head relative to the body
- Dark, blotchy grey colouring
- Eye set low on the head, like the silver carp
- Feeds mainly on zooplankton

Neither species has barbels.

---

## Black Carp (Mylopharyngodon piceus)

The black carp looks similar to a grass carp but is darker, often almost black, with dark fins. It eats mainly snails and mussels, which it crushes with strong teeth in its throat (pharyngeal teeth). It is an invasive species in parts of the USA, where it threatens native mussels.

---

## "Asian Carp" Explained

In North America, "Asian carp" means grass, silver, bighead and black carp together. These fish escaped from fish farms and ponds, spread through the Mississippi basin and have caused serious damage to native ecosystems. US agencies now use the term **"invasive carp"** instead.

In Britain and most of Europe, "Asian carp" isn't a term you'll often hear. The grass carp is the only one of the four that most European anglers will catch.

---

## Crucian Carp (Carassius carassius)

The crucian carp is a small, deep-bodied fish and a different genus from the common carp. Many anglers regard it as native to eastern England, and its numbers have fallen across much of its range because of habitat loss and hybridisation.

**How to identify it:**
- **No barbels**
- Deep, rounded body, often golden-bronze
- Long dorsal fin with an **outward-curving (convex) edge**
- Often a dark spot near the base of the tail in young fish
- Small: most are well under 1kg

**Crucian or goldfish?** The brown goldfish and the closely related Prussian carp (both *Carassius*) are often mistaken for crucians. They are usually greyer, with a dorsal fin that is straight or curves inward, and a more forked tail.

---

## Hybrids

Carp, crucian carp and goldfish can interbreed. Hybrids, often called F1s on commercial fisheries, are common.

**Signs of a hybrid:**
- Small or reduced barbels, sometimes only one pair
- A shape between a common carp and a crucian
- Features that don't quite match any one species

Hybrids are hard to identify with certainty from looks alone. If a fish doesn't fit any description cleanly, log it as a hybrid or "unconfirmed" rather than guessing.

## World Record Carp

"World record" is tricky for carp because there is no single authority every angler uses.

- The **biggest claimed carp** have come from heavily stocked, intensively fed commercial waters in continental Europe, especially Hungary and France. Some fish have been reported at over 45kg (100lb). Most weights in the angling press are claims and are rarely verified independently.
- The **IGFA** (International Game Fish Association) ratifies records under strict rules on tackle, weighing and witnesses. For many years its all-tackle record for common carp has been a fish of about 34kg (75lb) caught in France. Check the IGFA website for the current listing, as records change.
- In the UK, the British Record (rod-caught) Fish Committee keeps the national list.

Size depends far more on food supply, water temperature, stocking density and the strain of fish than on the scale pattern or the name of the lake.

## Handling Every Species Safely

Whatever the species, the same carp care rules apply:

- Keep fish wet and on a padded unhooking mat
- Hold them low over the mat, never standing up
- Support the body evenly and keep fingers out of the gills
- Return them quickly, holding them upright in the water until they swim off
